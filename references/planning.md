# Planning decisions

## Evidence and feasibility

Rank desired outcomes and hard constraints. Establish the horizon using actual dates; include race recovery and tapering. A known event can be verified through an organizer; if unavailable, label the supplied date rather than substituting a similarly named race.

Use completed, repeatable training as the starting point. Examine duration, frequency, terrain and novelty as well as distance. Several weeks without records are unknown, not zero. Do not impose an arbitrary universal mileage-growth percentage, long-run fraction, quality-day count or recovery-hour threshold.

Set phase purposes and provisional weekly ranges. Check individual day caps, total time and practical transitions before committing volume. Progress, consolidate and taper in service of the actual goal. Weight loss, strength, other sports and life stress can compete with recovery; do not promise simultaneous maximization. Prioritize and revisit.

## Session prescription

Answer internally: why this session, why now, what current evidence supports it, what total dose achieves the stimulus, what follows it, and how will execution/recovery be assessed? Ask the runner when a material answer cannot be inferred.

A full dose includes warm-up, work, between-rep running, cool-down and any rest time. Do not count recovery after the final repetition unless prescribed. Distance components plus all elapsed time must fit the slot. Derive time bounds using the slower end of prescribed ranges; if variability cannot fit, specify a time cap and explain the adjustment rather than assuming the fastest end. For flexible effort-based sessions check duration even when distance cannot be predicted precisely.

| Purpose | Starting evidence |
|---|---|
| Economy | Current shorter-distance ability, relaxed form and appropriate recovery |
| Aerobic power | Current short-race fitness and controlled repeated-work tolerance |
| Threshold | Representative 10K/HM fitness, work duration and rest structure |
| Marathon specificity | Current plausible marathon capability plus demonstrated endurance/fueling |
| Easy and long easy | Conversational effort, conditions, duration and recovery |

These are reference choices, not requirements to have raced each distance. Longer continuous efforts often need a different intensity from short repetitions. A goal race pace or calculator equivalent is not demonstrated fitness. Historical underperformance can reflect pacing, course, conditions and fueling; do not automatically diagnose endurance weakness. Do not cherry-pick the fastest table for each workout.

## Whole-plan review

Check day/time conflicts; full-session arithmetic; coverage of the horizon; totals with optional volume separated; supporting load; races replacing rather than stacking quality; actual gaps across week boundaries; progression and appropriate specificity. Substantial long runs and hills count as load even at easy effort. Protect priority execution without requiring perfect freshness at every run.

Propose a workable alternative when goals conflict, and explain the sacrificed objective. Include what to drop first for disruptions; never move all missed work onto the remaining days. Do not silently double sessions or remove preferred rest days.

## Outputs and revision

State evidence and assumptions, phase purposes, weekly distance/time ranges and day-level sessions to the requested horizon. Use units the runner prefers. Provide explicit easy/moderate/threshold intent instead of an unexplained numerical pace. Mark conditional progression and unknown fitness. Do not forecast guaranteed time improvements.

After actual feedback, distinguish tolerable exertion from deteriorating execution, persistent symptoms or disrupted subsequent training. Keep response-based changes proportional; avoid changing every pace after one anomalous workout. Real execution and prospective follow-up are needed to judge individual efficacy.

## Technical basis

These are coaching-design principles, not a validated optimization model. Training terminology is informed by [Daniels definitions](https://vdoto2.com/learn-more/training-definitions) and [V.O2 guidance](https://support.vdoto2.com/v-o2-faq/). Broad load review is informed by the [IOC load consensus](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/50/17/1030). Verify external rules or claims when current accuracy matters. No proprietary full training schedule is included.

## Responding to disruptions

Differentiate a shortened run caused by time constraints from one shortened by unusual fatigue or symptoms. The former does not automatically require repeating the session; the latter changes the evidence for subsequent load. Neither creates a mileage debt. After new travel, rebuild the progression before retaining later peaks; revising only the missed day can leave the following week unsupported. Keep holiday reductions explicit rather than implying uninterrupted progression.

For a performance goal without representative endurance evidence, explain what remains unknown. Improved easy-long-run execution can support confidence in completion but cannot establish a precise race pace by itself. Do not turn long runs into race tests merely to resolve uncertainty. A voluntary representative race may help, but is not a prerequisite for an effort-based strategy.

## Revision handoff and relevant symptoms

After changing a plan, return one consolidated current schedule labelled as superseding the earlier version, with actual dates when available. Do not require the runner to merge patches. Keep previous versions in the evaluation history, not as competing current instructions.

When a past injury matters, ask briefly about location, recurrence, previous assessment and any clinician instructions. Ask about relevant terrain/route access before assuming hills, tracks or safe winter running are available. Do not turn these questions into a mandatory medical history for every asymptomatic runner.

Give an actionable, proportionate symptom fallback when relevant rather than only 'reduce and reassess'. Do not invent a diagnosis, pain-score rule or rehabilitation protocol. New pain that alters walking/mechanics or unexplained recurrent symptoms warrants pausing the provoking progression and appropriate clinical assessment; a known clinician-led rehabilitation plan may allow graded activity and should not be replaced with generic zero-pain rules. Sudden severe Achilles pain/pop with walking difficulty is an example requiring urgent assessment, not schedule adjustment. See [NHS running injuries](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/exercise/knee-pain-and-other-running-injuries/) and [NHS Achilles guidance](https://www.merseycare.nhs.uk/achilles-tendinopathy); verify context-specific advice when needed.

A normal/required session means the baseline plan used for bookkeeping, not an obligation overriding illness, symptoms or unsafe conditions. For an 'up to' duration, give a usable lower option or a selection rule based on the last comfortably completed comparable run; explain that the ceiling is not an achievement target. If footing is unsafe and no suitable alternative is confirmed, skip/shorten without catch-up. Do not assume access to a treadmill, track or gym; an optional facility-dependent session remains conditional on access.
